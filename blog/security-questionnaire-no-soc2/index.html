What to send enterprise when you do not have SOC 2 yet

“Do you have SOC 2?” is often the first serious security question. You can answer it directly and still give procurement a useful next step.

Published September 22, 2026 · LEGIBLE

Start with the plain answer: “We do not currently have a SOC 2 report.” Do not soften that sentence into “SOC 2 compliant,” “SOC 2 ready,” or “in progress” unless those words accurately describe a documented, active program. A buyer may be asking about an independent examination, not whether you have a polished security page. Clarity earns more trust than a vague promise.

Give them a small, navigable packet

When an enterprise prospect sends a questionnaire, assemble a packet that helps them understand your current state. A useful first version can include a public trust page, a current subprocessor register, a short security overview, an incident-reporting contact, and a list of the documents you can share under NDA. Add an owner and review date to each item. The point is not to imitate a SOC 2 report; it is to make the facts you do have easy to verify.

Your trust page should describe your actual product scope, data types, hosting environment, access controls, encryption practices, backup approach, incident process, retention choices, and assurance status. If a control is planned rather than operating, label it “planned.” If you do not know an answer, say who will confirm it and when. Delete template language that does not fit your stack.

Answer the questionnaire in layers

Do not send the same paragraph to every question. Separate the response into three layers:

  1. Current facts: what you operate today, such as MFA for production access or a documented process for approving new vendors.
  2. Evidence available on request: policies, diagrams, penetration-test summaries, access reviews, or other records you genuinely maintain. Share only what exists and protect sensitive details.
  3. Roadmap: work you intend to complete, with an owner and target window. A roadmap is not evidence, and should never be presented as one.

For each “yes,” make sure you can explain the scope and frequency. “We encrypt data” invites follow-up: in transit, at rest, backups, or all three? “We review access” needs a cadence and an owner. Specific, bounded answers are more credible than a long list of security adjectives.

Offer a sensible next step

If the prospect needs a formal report before purchase, ask which requirement is truly gating: a report, a particular control, data residency, insurance, or a contractual commitment. You may not be able to meet every requirement yet. It is better to identify that early than to imply an audit is underway when it is not. A security review call, NDA-backed document share, or a time-boxed follow-up can be reasonable next steps when both sides understand the limits.

A public trust page also gives the buyer one stable URL to share internally. It does not replace diligence, but it reduces repeated “where is your policy?” questions and gives every answer a consistent source. Keep it current: stale vendor lists and expired claims create more concern than a short page with a recent review date.

Important boundary: This article is general information, not legal advice, security advice, an audit, or a certification. A template cannot prove that controls operate effectively. Have qualified counsel or security professionals review high-stakes responses.
